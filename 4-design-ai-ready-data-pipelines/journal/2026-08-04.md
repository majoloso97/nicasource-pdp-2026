### Journal
This article helped make the phrase "AI-ready data" feel a lot less "gray". Usually, when people say that, it sounds like they mean the data platform is modern, the pipelines are clean, or the data is accessible enough. The article makes the case that this is too vague to be useful, and I think that's correct overall.

The core idea is that AI-readiness is not really a static property of the data itself. It is more like a judgement about whether a certain data product is appropriate for a specific AI use case, under specific controls. A dataset might work perfectly well for internal summarization or trend analysis, but still be completely unsuitable for client-facing advice or automated decision-making.

Having experience in management system, their certification and audit processes, I found the proposed certification framing particularly useful because it frames an evidence-based process instead of an aspiration/idealization. Instead of saying "this data should be fine for AI", the better question becomes something like: what proof do we have that this product has the correct meaning, quality, ownership, etc, for the use case it is supposed to support? Funny enough, this framing also poses a challenge in maintaining high quality data. Organizations often struggle with the audit process, because certification requirements are often second-thought versus operational concerns.

What I like here is that the problem is not reduced to just data quality. I think a lot of teams (myself included) would default to completeness, freshness and accuracy when hearing "AI-ready". Those are important, yes, but they are not enough. If the business meaning is ambiguous, if there are no explicit usage constraints, or if AI systems can consume the product in inconsistent ways, then the data is not really ready regardless of how clean it looks. Looking back, I feel meaning is one that has affected the interactions I have with clients as often they don't realize the actual meaning of a piece of data in the business context.

Another strong point is the idea that certification cannot be self-declared. This feels specially relevant for AI systems, because they can amplify mistakes very quickly. A human analyst might notice an odd metric or know that a source should not be used in a certain context. An AI system does not have that organizational memory unless it has been made explicit somewhere in a machine-readable way. That makes the certification concept feel less like governance overhead, and more like a safety mechanism.

One practical implication I take from this is that AI-ready pipelines are not only about moving and transforming data. They also need to generate and preserve evidence: lineage, policy tags, semantic definitions, usage restrictions, observability signals and approval metadata. In that sense, the pipeline is not just delivering data, but also the assurance around that data.

Overall, this article refined my mental model around AI-ready data: Readiness should be closer in behavior to a certification based on evidence, continuously evaluated and preserved, and not just a badge we attach because a dataset looks mature enough.
